## Chapter 12: Full Exam Simulation & 100-Minute Pacing Strategy — Practice Questions

**Q1.** You are 25 minutes into the case-study section and realize the current question references an exhibit you have not opened yet. What should you do?

A) Re-read the entire scenario text again from the beginning to be safe
B) Open only the specific exhibit/tab the question references, then answer
C) Skip the question entirely and move to the next case study
D) Guess based on memory of the scenario summary without opening any exhibit

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

Case-study strategy is read-once, then reference only the specific exhibit a question calls out — this saves 3-5 minutes per question versus a full re-read.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Re-reading the full scenario wastes time you don't have in a 100-minute exam
- Skipping without flagging loses a guaranteed guess and doesn't fix the underlying time problem
- Guessing from memory alone risks missing exhibit-specific details the question is testing

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q2.** With 48 standalone items and 11 case-study items in a 100-minute exam, which pacing approach BEST protects your overall score?

A) Spend equal time (100/59 min) on every single item regardless of type
B) Finish all case studies first since they are worth more, then rush standalone items
C) Spend as long as needed on each standalone item since they are individually low-value
D) Budget ~1 minute per standalone item, reserving the remaining time for the case-study set and a final review buffer

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Standalone items are budgeted at ~1 min each (about 48 min), leaving roughly 52 min for the case-study set plus a review buffer — this matches the item mix, not a flat average.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- A flat average ignores that case-study items require scenario reading time, causing standalone time to be squeezed later
- Case studies are not inherently worth more per item; rushing standalone items risks avoidable losses on 1-minute questions
- Unlimited time per standalone item guarantees you run out of time before the case-study set

</details>

**Q3.** A standalone question reads: 'Which single approach BEST meets the requirements?' You are unsure between two plausible options with 20 seconds left in your allotted 1 minute. What should you do?

A) Leave the question blank and continue to save time
B) Spend an extra 3 minutes deliberating since accuracy matters more than pacing
C) Switch strategy and re-read the whole chapter's related exhibits before answering
D) Pick your best guess now, flag it, and move on to protect your per-item budget

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Flagging lets you commit an answer now (never leave blank) and return during final review if time allows, without breaking your per-item time budget.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Leaving it blank guarantees zero credit versus a 50/50 guess between two plausible options
- Spending 3 extra minutes cannibalizes time needed for remaining case-study items
- There are no additional exhibits for a standalone item, and re-reading unrelated material doesn't resolve the uncertainty

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q4.** A question is worded: 'Which combination of actions is the BEST single solution?' and presents four options, each listing one or two bundled steps. How should you interpret this wording?

A) Select the one option whose bundled steps together form the best single answer
B) Select exactly two of the four options since it says 'combination'
C) Select three options that together solve the problem
D) Treat it as invalid since combination questions aren't allowed

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

This exam format is single-answer only — 'combination' language means the correct option itself bundles multiple actions into one selectable choice, not that you pick multiple options.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Selecting two options treats it as a multi-select item, which this exam format does not use
- Selecting three options misapplies multi-select logic to a single-answer exam
- The wording is valid; it simply describes an option that contains a bundled multi-step action

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>

**Q5.** You have 8 minutes remaining and 3 flagged questions: one in 'Prepare and serve data', one in 'Implement and manage semantic models', and one in 'Maintain a data analytics solution'. Which should you review FIRST?

A) The 'Prepare and serve data' flag, since it carries the highest domain weight
B) The 'Maintain a data analytics solution' flag, since it appears earliest in the exam
C) Whichever flag you happen to click first
D) The 'Implement and manage semantic models' flag, since semantic models are hardest

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: A**

Domain-weight-based prioritization means fixing a flagged answer in the highest-weighted domain (Prepare and serve data, 45-50%) has the largest expected impact on your overall score.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Question order in the exam has no relationship to scoring impact
- Random selection ignores the weighted-impact strategy this chapter teaches
- Perceived difficulty of a topic doesn't correlate with its domain weight or scoring impact

</details>

**Q6.** For a scenario question with four plausible-looking options, which elimination approach is MOST effective under a 1-minute budget?

A) Read all four options in full detail multiple times before eliminating any
B) Pick the option that mentions the most services, since it looks the most complete
C) Pick the first option listed to save time
D) Immediately eliminate options that use deprecated services or fail to meet a stated hard constraint, then compare the remaining two

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: D**

Fast elimination targets options that violate explicit constraints or use outdated/wrong services first, narrowing four choices to two before spending remaining seconds on the final decision.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Multiple full re-reads of all four options burns the 1-minute budget without narrowing choices efficiently
- More services mentioned often signals an over-engineered, non-BEST-fit distractor
- Picking the first option ignores content entirely and has no strategic basis

</details>

**Q7.** What is the primary purpose of flagging a question during the exam rather than leaving it unanswered?

A) Flagging automatically awards partial credit
B) Flagging removes the question from your score calculation
C) Flagging guarantees a recorded best-guess answer while marking the item for a possible later revisit
D) Flagging is required by the exam software before you can proceed

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: C**

Flagging preserves your submitted best guess (avoiding a guaranteed zero) while tagging the item so you can return to it in the final review pass if time remains.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Flagging has no effect on scoring or partial credit mechanics
- Flagged items are still scored normally; flagging does not exclude them
- Flagging is optional and only used as a personal review aid, not a software requirement

</details>

**Q8.** During your final review checklist, you have 6 minutes left and flagged items spread across all three domains. Which action BEST reflects the recommended checklist approach?

A) Review flagged items in the order they were flagged, ignoring domain weight
B) Review flagged items proportionally by domain weight, spending the most time on 'Prepare and serve data' flags
C) Skip review entirely and submit immediately to avoid accidentally changing a correct answer
D) Re-answer every question in the exam from scratch within the remaining time

<details>
<summary>Answer</summary>

**Correct: B**

The final review checklist allocates remaining time proportionally to domain weight, prioritizing the domain worth the most points first to maximize expected score gain.

**Why the others are wrong:**
- Flag order has no correlation to scoring impact and ignores weight-based prioritization
- Skipping review forfeits a chance to fix guessed answers, which is the entire purpose of flagging
- Re-answering all 59 items in 6 minutes is not feasible and abandons the targeted flagged-review strategy

> ⚠️ Frequently missed on the exam — read the options carefully.

</details>
